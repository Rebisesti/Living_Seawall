RESEARCH

Wave action, storms, and rising sea levels are causing coastal shorelines to erode more quickly. As a result a lot of sea life is beginning to lose their homes, meaning we need to create new homes for them. However conventional seawalls may cause environmental damage due to the fact that they are frequently harsh, flat surfaces that cannot sustain marine life. There is a need for a modular, environmentally friendly seawall solution that reduces erosion while increasing habitat complexity that has Intertidal zones near urban shorelines that exhibit reduced biodiversity, with limited habitat for mussels, barnacles, seaweed, and other organisms.

Around the world, coastal erosion is a serious and expanding threat to infrastructure, ecosystems, and populations. Coastal land is steadily disappearing as a result of the natural balance of land and sea getting disrupted by rising sea levels and more severe weather brought on by climate change. According to U.S Climate Resilience Toolkit, coastal erosion is responsible for roughly $500 million per year in coastal property loss, including damage to structures and loss of land (U.S.CRT). In addition to putting houses, roads, and utilities in jeopardy, this process harms natural flood barriers including wetlands and marsh areas.

Engineers and environmental scientists are increasingly looking for creative and sustainable shoreline protection techniques to address the rising problem of coastal erosion and the deficiencies of conventional seawalls. Using eco-engineered panels or living seawalls, which are intended to preserve coastlines while boosting marine biodiversity, is our main goal. These structures incorporate textured surfaces and modular designs that mimic “natural rocky shorelines. 3D printing technology is used to create reusable molds of the panel designs. From this mould, concrete panels can be casted and manufactured in large numbers” (ICSI). We would be able to assemble them like building blocks which helps us with flexibility as well as installations and repairs.

Our weakness is the rapid population growth and migration. “This is a concern because population growth and the activities associated with it can degrade coastal and marine ecosystems” (Creel). Even though there is more economic opportunity, commercial access, and tourism from the migration, there is also a lot of environmental pressure involved. Therefore, if we can help find a way to create a seawall that helps slow down the speed of erosion, it will allow for more stable shorelines, save infrastructure, and the ecosystem which is crucial for economic and environmental reasons.

One of the best technical options for shielding coastal areas from erosion and flooding has long been thought to be traditional seawalls. These rigid constructions, which are mostly made of steel, concrete, or rock, serve as barriers that keep wave activity from reaching inland regions. Seawalls protect human infrastructure in the short term, but they also cause serious long-term environmental issues. Their rigid, vertical shape weakens the ecosystems they are intended to preserve by affecting sediment movement, destroying marine habitats, and interfering with natural coastal processes. The fundamental issue with traditional seawalls lies in how they interact with waves. Rather than absorbing wave energy, vertical seawalls “reflect wave energy, which can create intense hydrodynamic scour at the base of the wall where reflected energy meets incoming waves” (Pearce).

An example of an employed solution is a natural based seawall. “A living shoreline is a nature-based method for stabilizing a shoreline and protecting it from erosion. Living shorelines are sometimes referred to as green or soft shorelines. Unlike traditional “hard” shoreline stabilization techniques, such as concrete seawalls, living shorelines are made of natural materials such as plants, sand, rock, or oyster shells. Because they incorporate natural elements, living shorelines provide environmental benefits such as habitat for fish and wildlife. They can also become more stable over time, as the plants and roots grow.” (NOAA). Along with creating habitats that support marine biodiversity, the vegetation and biological characteristics help trap silt, reduce erosion, and absorb wave energy. This approach allows for greater resilience, as the living components can grow and adapt to changing sea levels over time.

Our mission is to find a medium balance between vertical seawalls and living seawalls. Both have their pros and cons. For example, “One of the most significant advantages of living shorelines is their ability to create and support diverse ecosystems. Native plants and submerged vegetation provide essential habitat for fish, crabs, and various bird species. These habitats contribute to increased biodiversity and healthier coastal ecosystems “ (AICP). However coastal lines are eroded way quicker. Then “Seawalls, however, offer no habitat value and can negatively impact local wildlife. They disrupt the natural flow of water and inhibit the growth of vegetation, reducing food and shelter availability for aquatic and terrestrial species” (AICP). But they are great at slowing down the rate of erosion. Finding a balance of both worlds helps create a more sustainable world.
